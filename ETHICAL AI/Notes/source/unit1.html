1. What is AI?

WhoDefinition
Kaplan & HaenleinA system's ability to correctly interpret external data, learn from it, and use that learning to achieve goals through flexible adaptation.
Poole & MackworthStudy of computational agents that act intelligently: actions fit circumstances & goals, flexible, learn from experience, choose well under limits.
Russell & NorvigStudy of agents that receive percepts and take actions — each agent is a function mapping percepts → actions.
  • Four schools (Russell & Norvig): machines that think like humans, act like humans, think rationally, act rationally.
  • Common core: designing intelligent agents that achieve goals. AI is a moving target — "AI is whatever computers can't do yet".
  • Autonomy is context-dependent (Greek auto-nomos, "self-rule"): engineering → works without an operator; bioethics → patient's right to decide; Kantian ethics → choosing one's own moral rules.
Why this "simple" question is hardNeeds speech recognition → language understanding (homophone lie) → world knowledge (dating, honesty) → a moral stance: consequentialist (maximise everyone's happiness), deontological (lying violates duty, Kant), virtue (be honest, Aristotle) — plus business risk for Apple.

1.2 Robots & why AI is hard

  • Robot = AI that is situated (real world) and embodied (physical body). Works by Sense → Plan → Act; an error at one step propagates to the next.
  • System integration (sensors/actuators with different failure modes) is one of the hardest parts.
  • Hard for AI: limited sensing; poor generalisation (face recogniser fails on a profile view); no common sense / common ground; no innate knowledge (a robot is a "blank slate"); repeated hype cycles.
  • Science vs fiction: HAL, Terminator, Westworld inflate expectations (the "Frankenstein complex"). Sophia (Saudi "citizenship", 2017) is scripted PR — no learning, no adaptation.

2. Strong vs Weak AI & Types of AI Systems

Turing Test (1950)If a human interviewer cannot tell a machine from a person in conversation, the machine is judged intelligent. Replaces the vague word "thinking" with a testable task. Criticism: no time limit specified; passing is not clearly necessary/sufficient for intelligence.
Weak (narrow) AIStrong AI (Searle, 1980)
ScopeOne narrowly defined taskGeneral intelligence across many problems
ClaimSimulates intelligent behaviourA suitably programmed computer has a mind like humans
StatusAll current AI (chess/Go engines, loan scoring, Siri)Not yet achieved

Types of AI systems: Expert systems (rule-based; e.g. mortgage advisors) • Planning systems (e.g. SPIKE for the Hubble telescope) • Computer vision (object recognition) • Machine learning.

3. Types of Ethics

Ethics = theory of morality (principles, general norms); morality = the actual rules, values and norms guiding action. Kant: ethics asks "What should I do?" (Greek ethos = custom; Cicero's mores → "moral").

Taxonomy of ethics (Ch. 3)
BranchQuestionKey points
DescriptiveWhat do people actually believe and do?Empirical — moral psychology, experimental economics. E.g. the ultimatum game: people give up profit for fairness. Gives input to normative ethics ("experimental ethics").
NormativeWhat ought to be done?Prescriptive; claims general validity ("stealing is wrong for everybody"). Three theories below.
Meta-ethicsWhat is morality itself?Ontology (what has moral worth), semantics (meaning of right/wrong/ought), epistemology (how moral truths are known). "Theory of ethics".
AppliedEthics in concrete fieldsMedicine, biotech, business. Influence runs both ways: practice shapes theory (solidarity means sharing in a family, fair competition in society).

3.1 The three normative theories

TheoryJudges an action byDetailCSR example
Deontological (duty ethics)The action itself — intention, duty, ruleKant's categorical imperative: act only on a maxim you can will as a universal law without contradiction.Critics ask about the company's motive (PR vs genuine)
ConsequentialistForeseeable consequencesBentham & Mill's utilitarianism — maximise happiness.Motive irrelevant; only the impact on society counts
Virtue ethicsThe agent's characterPlato's cardinal virtues (wisdom, justice, fortitude, temperance); Aristotle extends to 11 moral + intellectual virtues.Is the firm acting honestly and justly?

4. Relationship Between Ethics and Law

Popular view: "ethics starts where the law ends" — the book challenges it:

  1. Laws have an ethical side — anti-pollution and anti-trust laws are also ethical norms.
  2. Ethics works as "soft law" — firms follow ethical standards the law doesn't demand, to protect reputation and share price (e.g. fair-trade coffee as a selling point); the effect is almost like hard law.
RememberLaw = enforceable minimum; ethics = broader, often precedes and shapes law (e.g. hate-speech laws on social media). They overlap but are not opposites.

5. Machine Ethics

Machine ethicsAsks: what would it take to build an ethical AI that makes moral decisions? Machines lack phenomenology (feelings, consciousness) and moral intuition — they only process data about feelings. Critics (van Wynsberghe & Robbins) say roboticists haven't given strong reasons to build moral robots.
Sensors → Symbol grounding → Moral cognition (logic) → Action
  • Symbol grounding = converting raw sensor data into symbols (objects, events, properties, relations).
  • Logic = truth-preserving inference (Socrates is a man; all men are mortal ⇒ Socrates is mortal).

5.1 Machine ethics examples

Easy case — speeding-ticket robotRule: if driver X is speeding then the robot is obligated to issue a ticket to X. Fact: X is speeding ⇒ issue ticket. Clear rule + clear symbols = easy.
Hard case — toddler vs letterA robot on its way to post a letter sees a toddler fall into a 1 m stream. Two duties clash: rescue vs post. The robot has no empathy or sense of urgency, so it needs causal understanding and a utility scale: toddler = +1,000,000, on-time letter = +1 ⇒ rescue.
When naive utilitarian arithmetic failsNow the robot drives a truck with 1,000,001 letters (+1 each) ⇒ 1,000,001 > 1,000,000 ⇒ it lets the toddler drown. Simple utility sums give counter-intuitive results; implementing deontology needs a principled way to resolve clashes of duties.

6. Moral Diversity and Testing

  • Core problem: no agreement on the correct moral theory — polling (Bourget & Chalmers 2014): roughly ¼ of philosophers favour deontology, ¼ consequentialism, ⅓ virtue ethics. Which one do we implement?
  • Approach — test cases: design many ethical test cases; moral competence = ability to pass them. Iterating on new cases may expand competence and give insight into theory.
  • Testing & certifying fairness (active research): IEEE standards project on algorithmic bias (2017); AI Fairness 360, audit-AI; services like O'Neil Risk Consulting & Algorithmic Auditing; Facebook's Fairness Flow.
  • Also: codes of ethics for robotics engineers and HRI professionals. Some researchers remain pessimistic about machine morality.

7. Trust — the Foundation of the Core Principles

Trust (Lee & See 2004)The attitude that an agent will help achieve one's goals in a situation characterised by uncertainty and vulnerability.
  • Trust drives user acceptance: untrusted products fail. Culture matters — e.g. after the US-EU Safe Harbor was struck down (2015), cloud firms opened EU data centres to regain trust.
  • Functional elements: reliability, dependability, false-alarm rate, transparency, task complexity — performance & reliability dominate. Risk of injury/loss of life makes people reconsider trust.
  • Ethical elements: the five AI4People principles below. With AI it is unclear whether the machine decides or follows a script, so human experience of trust may mislead.

8. The Five AI4People Principles (Floridi et al. 2018)

PrincipleMeaning & examples
Non-maleficenceAI shall not harm people, animals or property. Online bullying (Sweden's 1993 law) and hate speech (Germany 2017: fines up to €50 M if illegal content isn't removed within a week — but risks over-removal vs free speech).
BeneficenceAI shall do good; benefits must outweigh harms (from bioethics): fewer AV accidents, elder-care robots, telemedicine, smart grids, protecting biodiversity, personalised education.
AutonomyAI shall respect people's goals and wishes. Allow informed risk-taking (Everest sherpas, rock climbing) but not harm to others (child tells a robot "pull my sister's hair"); be wary of euthanasia robots. Kant's three formulations: universal law • humanity as an end, never merely a means • kingdom of ends. Asimov's Three Laws alone don't make a Kantian ethical agent.
JusticeAI shall act justly and without bias → Section 9.
ExplicabilityIt must be possible to explain why an AI reached a result → Section 10.

9. Bias and Fairness (Principle of Justice)

  • Defining "justice" for a machine is hard because moral theory is contested; but in a narrow scope with clear laws, AI can make specific moral decisions.
  • Credit scoring: neighbourhood data → systematic bias against residential areas.
  • Healthcare algorithm (Obermeyer 2019): race excluded, yet cost used as proxy for need → poorer patients under-served; root cause = problem formulation.
  • COMPAS (courts): ProPublica (2016) found bias against African-American defendants; developers cited differing base rates. Chouldechova (2017): predictive parity and equal error rates cannot both hold when base rates differ → fairness definitions conflict, and there is a fairness – accuracy trade-off. Accuracy only ≈65%.
Detail in Unit 2Types of bias, detection metrics and mitigation techniques are covered in the Unit 2 notes.

10. Transparency and Explainability (Principle of Explicability)

  • Explicability = intelligibility + accountability (Floridi). It is not identical to transparency: publishing millions of lines of code won't help non-experts and may harm competitiveness.
  • Intelligibility: no inscrutable black box — someone can explain the workings to judges, juries and users.
  • Legal: EU GDPR "right to information/explanation" for algorithmic decisions — hard for neural networks → research in Explainable AI (XAI).
  • Justification is part of moral functioning. Loomis v. Wisconsin: defendant challenged the proprietary COMPAS score; appeal failed as judges did not rely on the score alone.
  • Accountability: log files (like an aircraft flight recorder, "black box") let investigators retrace steps and assign blame.

11. Privacy and Security +

Key ideas
PrivacyControl over personal data. AI is data-hungry; book Ch. 8 covers persistent surveillance, use of data for unintended purposes, auto-insurance discrimination, China's Social Credit System. Principles: consent, purpose limitation, data minimisation, anonymisation, GDPR rights.
SecurityProtect data and models from breaches, adversarial attacks, data poisoning and model theft. Failures destroy trust and cause harm (links to non-maleficence).

12. Robustness and Reliability +

  • Robustness: correct behaviour under changed, noisy or adversarial inputs — the book stresses AI generalises poorly and lacks common sense.
  • Reliability: consistent, dependable performance over time; the book names it (with performance) the dominant factor in trust. Failures in safety-critical domains (vehicles, medicine) risk life.
  • Practices: stress and edge-case testing, drift monitoring, fail-safe fallbacks, human oversight.

13. Quick Revision & Likely Questions

One-liners
  • AI = agents that perceive, learn and act towards goals; today's AI is all weak.
  • Ethics: descriptive (is) • normative (ought) • meta (nature) • applied (fields).
  • Normative: deontology (duty, Kant) • consequentialism (outcomes, utility) • virtue (character, Aristotle).
  • Ethics ≠ law but overlap; ethics acts as soft law.
  • Machine ethics hurdles: no feelings, no agreed theory, naive utilities misfire → test cases.
  • Five principles: Non-maleficence, Beneficence, Autonomy, Justice, Explicability.
